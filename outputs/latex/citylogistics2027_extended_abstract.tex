\title{From convenience asset to network lever: siting automated parcel
lockers and its impact on the local urban logistics scheme --- the case of
La Rochelle (France)}

\author[eigsi]{Corwin Fevre}
\author[eigsi]{Tatiana Graindorge}
\author[eigsi]{Robin David}
\author[eigsi]{Jean-Philippe Samier}
\address[eigsi]{EIGSI La Rochelle, 26 rue Vaux de Foletier, 17041 La Rochelle, France}

\begin{abstract}
Automated parcel lockers (APLs) are widely deployed to relieve the externalities
of e-commerce last-mile delivery, yet their value to the wider freight system is
decided less by the technology than by \emph{where lockers are placed}. This
paper asks how locker siting reshapes the \emph{local} urban logistics scheme of
a mid-sized, geographically peripheral city, pairing a state-of-the-art review
grounded in two professional freight diagnostics of La Rochelle (France) with a
multi-year field experimentation from three engineering projects (2021--2026). A
reproducible GIS multicriteria workflow, applied to 148 surveyed collection
points, the public-transport network and population data, yields ten
public-transport-anchored locker sites and exposes a gap between locker supply
and demand density. A companion survey classifies 95 public car parks and finds
74 suitable for micro-hub installation, showing that the land to anchor
consolidation near demand already exists. Coupling locker siting with this
consolidation and cyclo-logistics gives an estimated 40\% CO\textsubscript{2}
reduction over five years and a 44\% cut in truck-kilometres on a real carrier
perimeter. For ``end-of-line'' cities, locker networks deliver system value only
when co-designed with consolidation, not as stand-alone amenities.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
E-commerce has pushed business-to-consumer parcel flows into city centres faster
than urban freight systems have adapted, concentrating congestion, emissions,
kerbside conflict and failed deliveries in the streets least able to absorb them.
Automated parcel lockers answer part of this problem: as unattended, out-of-home
collection-and-delivery points (CDPs), they consolidate many drops into one
secured stop and let recipients collect along trips they already make.

The value a locker network returns to the system, however, is fixed at the
\emph{siting} stage. A locker placed for real-estate convenience adds an asset
without changing flows; one placed where demand, accessibility and the carrier
network coincide restructures rounds and creates the volume that makes
consolidated, low-emission distribution viable. Evidence comes mostly from large
metropolises, while mid-sized ``end-of-line'' cities --- peripheral to national
corridors, served by few national operators --- remain thinly documented. We ask:
\textbf{how does locker siting reshape the local urban logistics scheme of a
mid-sized city?} We answer it for La Rochelle ($\sim$170{,}000 inhabitants), whose
authority targets carbon neutrality by 2040 and regulates city-centre delivery
(06:00--11:00 windows; a goal of fully low-emission vehicles). The contribution is
a transferable, low-data siting method and evidence that PT-anchored siting plus
consolidation reshapes --- not merely supplements --- the local scheme.

\section{State of the art and local context}
\textbf{Locker siting.} Locker location is a facility-location problem balancing
coverage, demand density and multimodal accessibility against capital cost and
cannibalisation of existing CDPs [7]. Two solution families dominate: exact
optimisation of locker number, size and location [7], and GIS-based multicriteria
decision-making that ranks candidate sites on accessibility and demand [9]. Both
converge on a compact set of criteria --- proximity to public-transport (PT)
nodes and soft modes, residential and footfall density, insertion into daily
trips --- and report re-delivery cuts near 30\% and lower per-parcel emissions
than van home delivery [6, 8]. Empirical studies warn lockers are often sited for
car rather than transit access, limiting reach [8]; PT-anchored pilots (Valencia,
SPROUT; Paris, RATP--Pickup) are the corrective [refs 3--5]. We extend this logic
in two rarely-combined ways: a low-data, reproducible workflow for mid-sized
cities, and the joint siting of lockers \emph{and} the upstream consolidation land
that makes them effective.

\textbf{The La Rochelle freight system.} A 2018 FRETURB diagnostic quantified
$\sim$101{,}300 goods movements/week across the agglomeration, $\sim$2{,}290/day
in the historic centre, with e-commerce at 0.31 parcels/household/week
($\sim$25{,}300/week, $\sim$20\% of movements) concentrated on two poles [ref 1].
The territory relied on an electric consolidation centre (Elcidis, 2001--2018) and
72 delivery bays, $\sim$80\% undersized. A 2024 study typologised logistics
objects (urban delivery/service spaces including lockers; distribution spaces;
platforms; regional gateways) and characterised La Rochelle as an
``end-of-line'' market with few resident national operators [ref 2].

\section{Case study and method}
Three EIGSI field projects form one line of inquiry:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{P1 (2021), cargo-bike last mile} --- hyper-centre flow analysis
  (633 shops, 38 streets, $\sim$31\% pedestrian) and comparative
  CO\textsubscript{2} accounting (cargo-bike $\approx$13 vs.\ thermal van
  $\approx$220 g/km) [ref 3].
  \item \textbf{P2 (2024), locker siting} --- a reproducible GIS multicriteria
  workflow (Python/GeoPandas): 148 collection points (relays, agencies, 9
  lockers); enrichment with PT stops (Y\'elo, 1{,}414), bike-share and car parks;
  5-minute walk buffers; selection ranking PT proximity, then non-cannibalisation
  of relays, then demand density. Output: ten PT-anchored locker sites [ref 4].
  \item \textbf{P3 (2025--2026), VerDelivery + foncier survey} --- a consolidation
  scenario (peripheral car-park nano-hubs fed by light goods vehicles, cargo-bikes
  for the final leg) on a real carrier perimeter (Aunis Messagerie); and a
  classification of 95 public car parks by goods-vehicle compatibility (surface
  $\geq$ 3 m = candidate; surface $<$ 3 m = conditional; structured = excluded)
  [ref 5].
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}
\textbf{A visible coverage gap.} The surveyed network (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp}a) has
120 relay points but only 9 automated lockers, clustered on the centre and eastern
axis and weakly aligned with the densest quartiers; the Y\'elo network instead
offers 1{,}414 candidate anchor nodes across the demand surface.

\textbf{Siting.} The analysis returns ten PT-integrated, high-footfall,
under-served locations (railway station, Place de Verdun, university
campus/residences, Y\'elo interchanges), complementary to existing relays and
candidate micro-hubs. Corpus benchmarks: 24/7 access, fewer failed deliveries,
$\sim$6{,}200 parcels/yr per unit, $\sim$12 g CO\textsubscript{2}/parcel vs.\
$\sim$50 g for a van [refs 4--5].

\textbf{Land for consolidation.} The foncier survey (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp}b)
classifies 95 car parks: 74 surface sites meet the $\geq$ 3 m clearance light
goods vehicles need, 9 are conditional, 12 excluded; Espace Encan ($\approx$420
spaces) is the VerDelivery pilot. The ``no available land'' objection to
consolidation does not hold here.

\textbf{System-level impact.} Coupling locker siting with this consolidation and
cyclo-logistics (P3) yields $\approx$\textbf{40\% CO\textsubscript{2} reduction
over five years} (236$\to$128 tCO\textsubscript{2}e), a \textbf{44\% cut in
truck-km}, and $\sim$\euro 5{,}505/yr fuel savings on the studied perimeter
[ref 5]. Lockers act as demand anchors, pulling home deliveries into off-peak,
consolidated provisioning and justifying micro-/nano-hub + cargo-bike
distribution; anchoring them to the Y\'elo network materialises a
passenger--freight interface.

\section{Discussion}
Siting is the decisive lever: PT-anchored, density-aware placement maximises
utilisation and modal-shift potential, whereas sites chosen for availability alone
risk cannibalising relay points ($\sim$30\% of host-shop turnover) without net
gain. Barriers persist --- a durable home-delivery preference, insufficient
network density, capital cost ($\approx$\euro 1{,}000/yr installed;
\euro 3{,}000--30{,}000/unit), accessibility for reduced-mobility users, and
governance. The lesson for ``end-of-line'' cities is integration: with few
resident national operators, a locker network returns system benefits only when
co-designed with consolidation and local cyclo-logistics. With 74 compatible car
parks, the land to host that consolidation already exists --- the binding
constraint is institutional, not spatial.

\section{Conclusion}
The work links locker \emph{siting} to \emph{system-level impact} in a real
mid-sized case, bridging professional diagnostics and field experimentation, and
shows PT-anchored siting plus consolidation reshapes the local scheme rather than
merely adding capacity. Perspectives: a capacitated coverage/p-median optimisation
of the candidate lockers and car parks [7], post-deployment utilisation
monitoring, and integration into the agglomeration's logistics roadmap and
delivery-bay master plan.
